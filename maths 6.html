Arguments and Validity

Logical Deduction, Truth Table Testing, and Rules of Inference

1. What is an Argument?

🔍 Detective Analogy: A detective investigates a crime by gathering physical clues (premises). By applying rational deduction, the detective derives who committed the crime (conclusion). If the conclusion inescapably follows from true clues, the reasoning is sound. That is the exact function of a logical argument.
📖 Definition: An argument (or logical deduction) is an assertion that a given set of propositions \(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n\), called hypotheses (or premises), yields or implies another proposition \(q\), called the conclusion.

An argument consists of two structural parts:

ComponentRole in Argument
Premises (Hypotheses)The given assertions or evidence provided in support of a claim.
ConclusionThe final assertion claimed to follow necessarily from the premises.

Standard Example:

  • If you study hard, you will pass the exam.
  • You did not pass the exam.
  • Therefore, you did not study hard.

Letting \(p\): You study hard, and \(q\): You pass the exam, the argument is symbolized as:

\[ \begin{aligned} p \to q &\quad (\text{Premise 1}) \\ \neg q &\quad (\text{Premise 2}) \\ \hline \therefore \neg p &\quad (\text{Conclusion}) \end{aligned} \]

(The symbol \(\therefore\) reads "therefore".)

2. Concept of Validity

📖 Definition of Validity: An argument is VALID if and only if whenever all its premises are True, the conclusion MUST also be True.

Equivalently: An argument is valid if the conditional statement: \[(p_1 \land p_2 \land \dots \land p_n) \to q\] is a tautology.

An argument is INVALID (a fallacy) if there is at least one row in its truth table where all premises are True, but the conclusion is False.

⚠️ Vital Exam Distinction: Validity refers strictly to the logical structure of the argument, not to whether the premises are factual in the real world. An argument can have false premises and still be completely valid!

3. Testing Validity Using Truth Tables

To determine validity using a truth table:

  1. Construct columns for all premises and the conclusion.
  2. Identify the critical rows (rows where every premise evaluates to True).
  3. Inspect the conclusion in those critical rows:
    • If the conclusion is True in all critical rows → The argument is VALID.
    • If the conclusion is False in even one critical row → The argument is INVALID.

Testing: \(p \to q, \; \neg q \; \therefore \neg p\)

\(p\) \(q\) Premise 1: \(p \to q\) Premise 2: \(\neg q\) Conclusion: \(\neg p\) Evaluation
TT TF F Not a critical row (Premise 2 is F)
TF FT F Not a critical row (Premise 1 is F)
FT TF T Not a critical row (Premise 2 is F)
FF TT T CRITICAL ROW (Both premises T → Conclusion T)

In Row 4, both premises are True, and the conclusion \(\neg p\) is also True. Since there is no row where the premises are True and the conclusion is False, the argument is VALID.

4. Formal Rules of Inference

Rules of inference are standard, universally valid deductive forms used to construct proofs without drawing large truth tables:

1. Modus Ponens (Law of Detachment) \(p \to q, \; p \vdash q\)

If an implication is true and its hypothesis is true, its conclusion must be true.

\[ \begin{aligned} p \to q \\ p \\ \hline \therefore q \end{aligned} \] Memory rule: Confirm the condition → Confirm the result.
2. Modus Tollens \(p \to q, \; \neg q \vdash \neg p\)

If an implication is true and its conclusion is false, its hypothesis must be false.

\[ \begin{aligned} p \to q \\ \neg q \\ \hline \therefore \neg p \end{aligned} \] Memory rule: Deny the result → Deny the condition.
3. Principle of Syllogism (Hypothetical Syllogism) \(p \to q, \; q \to r \vdash p \to r\)

Chains two conditional statements together transitively.

\[ \begin{aligned} p \to q \\ q \to r \\ \hline \therefore p \to r \end{aligned} \]
4. Principle of Adjunction (Conjunction Introduction) \(p, \; q \vdash p \land q\)

If two separate statements are true independently, their conjunction is true.

\[ \begin{aligned} p \\ q \\ \hline \therefore p \land q \end{aligned} \]
5. Principle of Simplification \(p \land q \vdash p\)

If a conjunction is true, either of its conjuncts can be deduced as true.

\[ \begin{aligned} p \land q \\ \hline \therefore p \end{aligned} \]
6. Modus Tollendo Ponens (Disjunctive Syllogism) \(p \lor q, \; \neg p \vdash q\)

Given an alternative where at least one is true, denying one forces the other to be true.

\[ \begin{aligned} p \lor q \\ \neg p \\ \hline \therefore q \end{aligned} \]
7. Modus Ponendo Tollens (Exclusive Disjunction) \(\neg(p \land q), \; p \vdash \neg q\)

If two statements cannot both be true simultaneously, affirming one eliminates the other.

\[ \begin{aligned} \neg(p \land q) \\ p \\ \hline \therefore \neg q \end{aligned} \]
8. Constructive Dilemma \((p \to r) \land (q \to s), \; p \lor q \vdash r \lor s\)

Combines two conditional statements with a disjunction of their antecedents.

\[ \begin{aligned} (p \to r) \land (q \to s) \\ p \lor q \\ \hline \therefore r \lor s \end{aligned} \]
9. Principle of Equivalence \(p \equiv q\)

Any statement or part of a statement may be replaced by a logically equivalent expression without changing the argument's validity (e.g., replacing \(p \to q\) with \(\neg p \lor q\)).

10. Principle of Conditionalization (Conditional Proof)

To establish a conditional conclusion \(p \to q\), assume \(p\) as an auxiliary premise. If \(q\) is derived logically from this assumption, then \(p \to q\) is validly established.

5. Formal Proof of Validity

A formal proof is a finite sequence of numbered statements such that each statement is either a premise or follows from earlier lines via a valid rule of inference, ending with the conclusion.

Deduction Example:

Given the premises: \(p \to q\), \(q \to r\), and \(p\). Prove the conclusion: \(r\).

StepStatementJustification
1\(p \to q\)Premise
2\(q \to r\)Premise
3\(p\)Premise
4\(q\)Modus Ponens from steps (1, 3)
5\(r\)Modus Ponens from steps (2, 4)

The conclusion \(r\) has been formally deduced as valid.

6. Classic Valid Forms vs. Common Fallacies

Argument FormSymbolic StructureStatus
Modus Ponens \(p \to q, \; p \;\therefore q\) VALID
Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent \(p \to q, \; q \;\therefore p\) INVALID (Fallacy)
Modus Tollens \(p \to q, \; \neg q \;\therefore \neg p\) VALID
Fallacy of Denying the Antecedent \(p \to q, \; \neg p \;\therefore \neg q\) INVALID (Fallacy)
⚠️ Exam Trap:
• Affirming the condition (\(p\)) validly gives the result (\(q\)). But confirming the result (\(q\)) does NOT prove \(p\) happened (the patient could have passed for other reasons).
• Denying the result (\(\neg q\)) validly denies the condition (\(\neg p\)). But denying the condition (\(\neg p\)) does NOT prove \(\neg q\).
