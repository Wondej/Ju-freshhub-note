The Five Logical Connectives

Compound Propositions, Truth Tables, and Logical Operators

In propositional logic, simple statements can be modified or connected using logical connectives (operators) to construct compound propositions.

🔢 Mathematical Operator Analogy: Just as arithmetic operators (\(+\), \(-\), \(\times\)) act on numbers to produce new numerical values, logical connectives act on truth values of propositions to generate new propositions.

Consider two atomic propositions:

  • \(p\): Jimma is in Ethiopia. (True)
  • \(q\): Addis Ababa is the capital of Ethiopia. (True)

There are five fundamental logical connectives used to manipulate these statements.

1. Negation (NOT)

The negation of a proposition \(p\), denoted by \(\neg p\) (or \(\sim p\)), reverses the original truth value of the proposition.

Example:

  • \(p\): \(2\) is an even number. (True)
  • \(\neg p\): \(2\) is NOT an even number. (False)

Truth Table for Negation:

\(p\)\(\neg p\)
TF
FT
💡 Memory Rule: \(\neg p\) always yields the exact opposite truth value of \(p\).

2. Conjunction (AND)

The conjunction of propositions \(p\) and \(q\), denoted by \(p \land q\), is the compound proposition formed by connecting them with AND.

Example:

  • \(p\): \(4\) is even. (T)
  • \(q\): \(5\) is odd. (T)
  • \(p \land q\): \(4\) is even AND \(5\) is odd. (T)
📌 The Conjunction Rule: \(p \land q\) is TRUE only when BOTH \(p\) and \(q\) are TRUE. In all other cases, it is False.

Truth Table for Conjunction:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \land q\)
TTT
TFF
FTF
FFF
🎓 Academic Analogy: Suppose passing a pre-medical entrance examination requires passing Biology AND Chemistry. If a candidate fails either one (or both), the requirement is not met.

3. Disjunction (OR)

The disjunction of propositions \(p\) and \(q\), denoted by \(p \lor q\), is the compound proposition formed by connecting them with OR.

Example:

  • \(p\): \(4\) is even. (T)
  • \(q\): \(5\) is even. (F)
  • \(p \lor q\): \(4\) is even OR \(5\) is even. (T)
📌 The Disjunction Rule: \(p \lor q\) is FALSE only when BOTH \(p\) and \(q\) are FALSE. If at least one statement is true, the compound proposition is True.

Truth Table for Disjunction:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \lor q\)
TTT
TFT
FTT
FFF
⚠️ Inclusive OR vs. Exclusive OR: In formal mathematical logic, the connective "OR" (\(\lor\)) is inclusive. This means \(p \lor q\) is True when \(p\) is true, when \(q\) is true, and also when both are true simultaneously.

4. Implication / Conditional (IF... THEN)

The conditional statement (implication), denoted by \(p \to q\) (or \(p \implies q\)), represents the statement: "If \(p\), then \(q\)".

  • \(p\) is called the hypothesis (or antecedent / premise).
  • \(q\) is called the conclusion (or consequent).

Example:

"If a number is divisible by \(4\), then it is even."

  • \(p\): A number is divisible by \(4\).
  • \(q\): It is even.
📌 The Implication Rule: \(p \to q\) is FALSE in exactly one case: when \(p\) is TRUE and \(q\) is FALSE (\(\text{T} \to \text{F}\)). In all other three cases, it evaluates to True.

Truth Table for Implication:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \to q\)
TTT
TFF
FTT
FFT
🤝 The Broken Promise Analogy:
A father promises his son: "If you pass the university entrance exam (\(p\)), then I will buy you a smartphone (\(q\))."

• T → T: The son passes; the father buys the phone → Promise kept (True).
• T → F: The son passes; the father fails to buy the phone → Promise broken (False).
• F → T: The son does not pass; the father buys the phone anyway → Promise not broken (True).
• F → F: The son does not pass; the father does not buy the phone → Promise not broken (True).

Alternative Linguistic Expressions for \(p \to q\):

  • "If \(p\), then \(q\)"
  • "\(p\) implies \(q\)"
  • "\(p\) is sufficient for \(q\)"
  • "\(q\) is necessary for \(p\)"
  • "\(p\) only if \(q\)"
  • "\(q\) if \(p\)"

5. Bi-implication / Biconditional (IF AND ONLY IF)

The bi-implication, denoted by \(p \leftrightarrow q\) (or \(p \iff q\)), represents: "\(p\) if and only if \(q\)" (abbreviated as "\(p\) iff \(q\)").

📌 The Bi-implication Rule: \(p \leftrightarrow q\) is TRUE when both \(p\) and \(q\) have the SAME truth value (both True or both False). It is False when they have opposite truth values.

Truth Table for Bi-implication:

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \leftrightarrow q\)
TTT
TFF
FTF
FFT

Example:

  • \(p\): \(3\) is odd. (T)
  • \(q\): \(2\) is prime. (T)
  • Since both propositions are True, \(p \leftrightarrow q\) is True.
