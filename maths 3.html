Compound Propositions and Logical Equivalence

Truth Tables, Conditional Variations, and Fundamental Logical Laws

1. What is a Compound Proposition?

📖 Definition: A compound proposition is a proposition formed by combining two or more simple (atomic) propositions using one or more logical connectives.

Consider three atomic propositions:

  • \(p\): \(4\) is an even number. (\(\text{True}\))
  • \(q\): \(7\) is a prime number. (\(\text{True}\))
  • \(r\): \(10\) is an odd number. (\(\text{False}\))

Now evaluate the compound expression:

\((p \land q) \to r\)

Read as: "If \(4\) is even AND \(7\) is prime, THEN \(10\) is odd."

Step-by-Step Evaluation:
• Step 1: Evaluate inside the parentheses: \(p \land q = \text{T} \land \text{T} = \text{T}\)
• Step 2: Substitute the truth value: \(\text{T} \to r \implies \text{T} \to \text{F}\)
• Step 3: Apply the implication rule (\(\text{T} \to \text{F}\) is False):
\(\mathbf{(p \land q) \to r = \text{False}}\)
💡 Problem-Solving Rule: Never attempt to evaluate a multi-operator compound expression in a single step. Isolate components inside parentheses first, evaluate intermediate steps, and combine outward.

2. Construction of Truth Tables

A truth table enumerates every possible combination of truth values for constituent atomic variables:

  • \(1\) proposition (\(p\)) produces \(2^1 = 2\) rows.
  • \(2\) propositions (\(p, q\)) produce \(2^2 = 4\) rows.
  • \(3\) propositions (\(p, q, r\)) produce \(2^3 = 8\) rows.
  • In general, \(n\) distinct propositions produce \(2^n\) rows.

Standard 4-Row Setup for Two Variables:

\(p\)\(q\)

FF

Truth Table Construction for \(p \land \neg q\):

To evaluate \(p \land \neg q\), first create an intermediate column for \(\neg q\), then compute the conjunction with \(p\):

\(p\)\(q\)\(\neg q\)\(p \land \neg q\)

3. Order of Operations and Parentheses

The placement of parentheses alters the grouping and final truth evaluation of an expression:

\((p \land q) \lor r \quad \not\equiv \quad p \land (q \lor r)\)

🔢 Arithmetic Precedence Analogy:
• \((2 + 3) \times 4 = 5 \times 4 = 20\)
• \(2 + (3 \times 4) = 2 + 12 = 14\)
Parentheses dictate precedence in logic exactly as they do in arithmetic!

Precedence Convention:

When parentheses are omitted by convention:

  1. Negation (\(\neg\)) has the highest precedence.
  2. Conjunction (\(\land\)) and Disjunction (\(\lor\)) take precedence over Implication (\(\to\)) and Bi-implication (\(\leftrightarrow\)).

Best Practice: Always use parentheses explicitly to prevent ambiguity during examination calculations.

4. Logical Equivalence

📖 Definition: Two compound propositions \(P\) and \(Q\) are logically equivalent, denoted by \(P \equiv Q\) (or \(P \iff Q\)), if and only if they have identical truth values across every row of their truth tables.

Proving That \(p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q\):

Construct a comparative truth table and inspect the resulting columns:

\(p\)\(q\)\(\neg p\)\(p \to q\)\(\neg p \lor q\)
TTFTT
TFFFF
FTTTT
FFTTT

Since the column for \(p \to q\) and the column for \(\neg p \lor q\) match in every single row:

\(\mathbf{p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q}\)

⚠️ Equivalence Criteria: Two statements are equivalent only if they match across all \(2^n\) rows. If even a single row differs, they are not logically equivalent.

5. Variations of the Conditional Statement

Given an original conditional statement: \(p \to q\)

"If it rains (\(p\)), then the ground becomes wet (\(q\))."

The Three Associated Conditionals:

  • Converse (\(q \to p\)): Switch the hypothesis and conclusion.
    "If the ground is wet, then it rains."
  • Inverse (\(\neg p \to \neg q\)): Negate both without switching positions.
    "If it does not rain, then the ground does not become wet."
  • Contrapositive (\(\neg q \to \neg p\)): Switch positions AND negate both.
    "If the ground does not become wet, then it does not rain."

⭐ The Golden Law:
• An implication is always logically equivalent to its contrapositive: \((p \to q) \equiv (\neg q \to \neg p)\).
• An implication is NOT generally equivalent to its converse or inverse: \((p \to q) \not\equiv (q \to p)\).
🧠 Memory Summary:
• Converse = Switch.
• Inverse = Negate.
• Contrapositive = Switch + Negate.

6. Fundamental Laws of Logical Equivalence

These standard equivalences allow compound propositions to be simplified algebraically without calculating full truth tables:

Law CategoryEquivalence Identity
Idempotent Laws \(p \lor p \equiv p\)
\(p \land p \equiv p\)
Commutative Laws \(p \lor q \equiv q \lor p\)
\(p \land q \equiv q \land p\)
Associative Laws \((p \lor q) \lor r \equiv p \lor (q \lor r)\)
\((p \land q) \land r \equiv p \land (q \land r)\)
Distributive Laws \(p \lor (q \land r) \equiv (p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)\)
\(p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)\)
De Morgan's Laws \(\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q\)
\(\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q\)
Contrapositive Law \(p \to q \equiv \neg q \to \neg p\)
Double Negation Law \(\neg(\neg p) \equiv p\)
Complement Laws \(p \lor \neg p \equiv \mathbf{T}\) (Tautology)
\(p \land \neg p \equiv \mathbf{F}\) (Contradiction)
Identity Laws \(p \land \mathbf{T} \equiv p\)
\(p \lor \mathbf{F} \equiv p\)
Domination Laws \(p \lor \mathbf{T} \equiv \mathbf{T}\)
\(p \land \mathbf{F} \equiv \mathbf{F}\)

Intuitive Understanding of De Morgan's Laws:

1. "It is NOT true that I study Biology AND Chemistry."
• Means I do not study Biology OR I do not study Chemistry: \(\mathbf{\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q}\)

2. "It is NOT true that I study Biology OR Chemistry."
• Means I study neither subject: \(\mathbf{\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q}\)

💡 De Morgan's Operation Rule: When negation (\(\neg\)) distributes into parentheses, it negates every term and flips the operator (\(\land\) becomes \(\lor\), and \(\lor\) becomes \(\land\)).
